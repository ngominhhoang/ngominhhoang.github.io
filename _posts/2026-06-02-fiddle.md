*A walk through the ideas behind FIDDLE, our framework for compiling more reliable quantum circuits, and why minimizing circuit depth alone can leave real reliability on the table.*

Before any quantum algorithm runs on real hardware, it needs to be compiled. This step, called transpilation, takes the abstract operations required by an algorithm and turns them into something a specific noisy device can execute. It usually involves three stages: layout, routing, and translation. Layout assigns logical qubits to physical qubits. Routing orders the gates and inserts SWAP operations so that two-qubit gates act only on connected hardware qubits. Translation rewrites the circuit into the native gate set of the device.

The routing stage is where a lot of reliability is quietly gained or lost. Hardware qubits are not all connected to each other, so the compiler often has to move quantum information around using SWAP gates. Every extra SWAP adds more operations, and more operations usually mean more noise. A poor routing decision can therefore make a circuit much less reliable, even if the original algorithm is unchanged.

This is the problem FIDDLE studies. The main question is simple: how should we route a quantum circuit if the goal is to make the final circuit as faithful as possible to the ideal computation?

## The problem with proxy metrics

If we care about reliability, we should optimize reliability directly. However, many routing methods optimize simpler quantities such as circuit depth or total gate count. These are useful proxies. In general, a circuit with fewer gates and smaller depth is less exposed to noise. But depth and gate count are not the same as reliability.

Two circuits can have the same depth but different fidelity, depending on which gates are placed on which qubits and how the device noise affects them. This matters on real quantum hardware, where qubits and gates are not equally reliable.

The reason people use proxies is not that fidelity is unimportant. The reason is that fidelity is expensive to compute. In particular, process fidelity is a much more faithful reliability metric, but evaluating it directly inside a search loop is computationally difficult. A routing method may need to compare many candidate circuits, so calling an expensive fidelity routine every time is not practical.

FIDDLE tries to close this gap. Instead of optimizing depth or gate count and hoping fidelity improves, it builds a way to optimize process fidelity more directly.

## Why process fidelity matters

There are several ways to measure circuit reliability. Some cheaper metrics, such as Probability of Successful Trial and Hellinger Fidelity, only compare measurement outcomes in the computational Z basis. This can miss important differences between quantum states.

For example, these metrics cannot distinguish the plus superposition of 0 and 1 from the minus superposition of 0 and 1, because both produce the same outcome distribution when measured in the Z basis. The difference is in the phase, and phase is essential in quantum computation.

Process fidelity gives a more complete view. It measures how close the noisy circuit is to the ideal quantum process, rather than only comparing one measurement distribution. That makes it a better target when the goal is circuit reliability.

The challenge is cost. Exact process fidelity is hard to compute, and practical estimation methods are still expensive. This creates the central difficulty: to optimize fidelity, we need to evaluate fidelity many times, but evaluating fidelity many times is exactly what we cannot afford.

## Learning the shape of fidelity

FIDDLE addresses this using a surrogate model. The idea is to learn a fast approximation of process fidelity from a limited number of expensive fidelity labels.

The first step is to represent each circuit in a continuous latent space. We use a temporal graph convolutional autoencoder for this. The graph part captures the hardware connectivity between qubits. The temporal part captures the layer-by-layer structure of the circuit. Instead of training this representation directly on process fidelity, which is expensive to label, we train it using cheaper circuit traits such as circuit depth and gate counts.

This helps organize the latent space. Circuits with similar structural and fidelity-related traits are placed near each other. Once we have this space, predicting fidelity becomes easier.

The second step is to use a Gaussian Process model in the latent space. This is useful because process fidelity labels are limited. A Gaussian Process can work well with fewer samples and also gives uncertainty estimates. That uncertainty is helpful because it tells us when the surrogate is less confident.

In short, the surrogate model makes an expensive metric cheap enough to use during optimization.

## Using reinforcement learning for routing

Once we can estimate fidelity quickly, routing can be treated as a reinforcement learning problem.

The RL agent builds a gate sequence one decision at a time. At each step, it chooses which gate or SWAP operation to place next. The state contains information about the remaining gates and the current partial solution. The reward is designed to encourage valid, compact, and high-fidelity circuits.

Most importantly, the final reward depends on the estimated process fidelity of the completed circuit. This is the key difference. The agent is not trained only to reduce depth or gate count. It is trained to improve the metric we actually care about.

There are also practical details that matter. SWAP gates receive a small penalty because they add noise. Placing useful gates receives a small reward. Invalid or overly long solutions are penalized. The framework is also designed so that the agent does not get stuck in states where no progress is possible.

## What we found

We tested FIDDLE on QAOA and quantum machine learning circuits with 5 and 7 qubits. The experiments used several noise models, including depolarizing noise, bit-flip noise, phase-flip noise, mixed noise, and a realistic hardware noise model.

The results show two clear patterns.

First, the surrogate model estimates process fidelity more accurately than the learning-based baseline when only limited fidelity labels are available. This is important because process fidelity labels are expensive, so label efficiency is a central part of the problem.

Second, the full FIDDLE framework produces higher-fidelity routed circuits than the compared routing methods across the tested noise models. The advantage becomes more visible on larger circuits, where routing decisions have a stronger effect on reliability. On the harder 7-qubit settings, the improvement over the GNN-based competitor reaches double digits in some cases.

These results support the main message of the work: optimizing a better reliability metric can lead to better circuits, especially when the hardware noise is realistic and the routing problem becomes harder.

## A useful lesson from bit-flip noise

One interesting observation appears under bit-flip noise. In this setting, the relationship between circuit structure and fidelity becomes less clean. Normally, adding more SWAPs tends to reduce fidelity because SWAPs introduce more gates and more noise. Under bit-flip noise, however, some errors can interact with the circuit in less predictable ways. As a result, circuits with different structures may end up with more similar fidelity than expected.

This makes fidelity prediction harder. It also shows why reliability-aware routing is more subtle than simply counting gates. The type of noise matters, and different noise models can change how circuit structure relates to final fidelity.

## The takeaway

The broader lesson is simple: when the metric we care about is expensive, it is tempting to optimize a cheaper proxy. But the proxy can quietly change the objective.

FIDDLE shows that we do not always have to settle for that compromise. By learning a surrogate for process fidelity and using it inside an RL-based routing framework, we can optimize circuit reliability more directly. As quantum hardware scales and circuits become deeper, this kind of fidelity-aware compilation will become increasingly important.

The goal is not just to produce shorter circuits. The goal is to produce circuits that survive noise better.
