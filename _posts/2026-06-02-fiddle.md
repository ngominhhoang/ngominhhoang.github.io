*A walk through the ideas behind FIDDLE, our framework for compiling more reliable quantum circuits, and why "just minimize the circuit depth" leaves real reliability on the table.*

Before any quantum algorithm runs on real hardware, it gets compiled. This step, called transpilation, takes the abstract list of operations an algorithm needs and turns it into something a specific noisy device can actually execute. It happens in three stages: **layout** (assigning the algorithm's logical qubits to physical ones), **routing** (ordering the gates and inserting SWAP operations so that two-qubit gates only act on physically adjacent qubits), and **translation** (rewriting everything into the hardware's native gate set).

The routing stage is where a lot of reliability is quietly won or lost. Hardware qubits aren't all connected to each other, so to make a gate possible the compiler often has to SWAP qubits around, and every SWAP is extra operations, which means extra noise. Route badly and you bury your computation under avoidable errors. So the natural question is: how should the compiler route a circuit to make it as reliable as possible?

This is the question FIDDLE tackles. And the most interesting part turned out to be confronting a gap between what the field *optimizes* and what it actually *cares about*.

## Insight 1: We've been optimizing a stand-in, not the real thing

If you want reliable circuits, you should optimize for reliability. But almost every routing method optimizes something else, usually circuit depth or total gate count. Those are reasonable proxies: fewer, shallower operations generally means less noise. But "generally" is doing a lot of work. Depth and gate count are *correlated* with reliability, but they aren't the same thing. Two circuits with identical depth can have meaningfully different reliability depending on which gates land on which noisy qubits.

The reason everyone reaches for proxies isn't laziness. It's cost. The honest reliability metric is expensive to compute, so optimizing it directly inside a search loop has been impractical. FIDDLE's goal was to close that gap: optimize the real metric, directly.

## Insight 2: The "real" metric is more honest — and far more expensive

There are a few ways to measure how reliable a circuit is. The cheap ones, Probability of Successful Trial (PST) and Hellinger Fidelity (HF), only look at measurements in the computational (Z) basis. That sounds fine until you realize it makes them blind to phase. They literally cannot tell the state |0&#10217; + |1&#10217; apart from |0&#10217; - |1&#10217;, because both give the same outcome distribution when you measure in the Z basis. For quantum computation, where phase carries real information, that blindness matters.

**Process Fidelity (PF)** fixes this by comparing the ideal and actual states across all measurement bases, not just one. It's the trustworthy metric. The catch: computing it exactly is QSZK-hard, and even good estimation methods are slow. So we have an awkward situation. The metric worth optimizing is the one you can't afford to evaluate very often. An RL agent that needs to score thousands of candidate routings simply cannot call a slow fidelity routine every time.

This is the chicken-and-egg problem at the center of the paper: to optimize fidelity you need to evaluate it constantly, but evaluating it constantly is exactly what you can't do.

## Insight 3: Make an expensive metric cheap by learning its shape

The way out is a **surrogate model**, a fast stand-in that predicts process fidelity well enough to guide a search, trained on only a small number of the expensive true labels.

The trick to making that work with limited data is to organize circuits cleverly before predicting anything. We train an autoencoder (a temporal graph convolutional network, which captures both the hardware's qubit-connectivity structure and the layer-by-layer flow of the circuit) to embed each circuit into a continuous latent space. Crucially, we train it against **cheap-to-compute traits**. For example, things like circuit depth and per-type gate counts correlate with fidelity but cost almost nothing. The effect is that circuits with similar fidelity end up near each other in the latent space.

Once the space is organized that way, predicting fidelity becomes smooth interpolation, and that's where a **Gaussian Process (GP)** shines. A GP can give accurate predictions from a handful of true fidelity measurements, and it reports its own uncertainty, so you know where its predictions are shaky. We also pick *which* few circuits to label using an informativeness criterion that's submodular, which gives a provable near-optimal guarantee on how useful the chosen training set is.

The payoff: against a graph-neural-network fidelity estimator from prior work, our GP surrogate cut prediction error by roughly 1.5× to 2.5×. The earlier GNN approach works fine for the *cheap* metrics (you can generate mountains of training labels), but for process fidelity, where labels are scarce and precious, learning from limited data is the whole game, and the GP wins it.

## Insight 4: Now let reinforcement learning do the searching

With a fast fidelity estimate in hand, the search itself becomes a reinforcement learning problem. We frame routing as building a gate sequence one step at a time: the agent's state is the set of gates still to place plus the partial sequence so far, an action picks the next gate, and the reward uses the surrogate's fidelity estimate.

The reward shaping is where some hard-won lessons live. SWAPs get a small penalty (they add noise, so don't insert them gratuitously); placing a needed gate earns a small bonus; blowing past the gate budget is penalized; and the big payoff is delayed until the end, proportional to the estimated fidelity of the finished circuit. We also made sure a valid SWAP move always exists at every step. That is a small detail that fixes a real failure mode in earlier RL routing work, where the agent could get stuck in a state that never changes and loop forever.

## What we found

Across QAOA and quantum machine learning circuits, on both 5- and 7-qubit systems, and under five noise models, including one replicating a real Google processor, FIDDLE consistently produced higher-fidelity circuits than the baselines we compared against, including Qiskit's routing and methods specialized for these circuit families.

Two patterns stood out. First, FIDDLE's advantage *grew* with circuit size, exactly where reliability is hardest to maintain and matters most. Second, the gains were largest under the realistic hardware-noise model, which is the setting you actually care about. On the harder problems the improvements over a GNN-based competitor reached double digits in percentage points.

## A wrinkle worth sharing: when noise imitates a SWAP

One finding was a genuine surprise. Under bit-flip noise, FIDDLE's fidelity predictions were noticeably harder, and the usual tidy relationship between circuit structure and fidelity got muddier. The reason is a small piece of physics: a bit-flip hitting two qubits can act like a SWAP. So a circuit stuffed with SWAPs can, by accident, partially cancel bit-flip errors. It means that two circuits that look very different (different depths, different gate counts) can end up with surprisingly similar fidelity. That breaks the "similar structure, similar fidelity" assumption the surrogate leans on, and it's a clean reminder that error in quantum systems doesn't always behave like generic, structure-agnostic noise.

## The takeaway

The broad lesson outlasts this one framework: **when the metric you truly care about is expensive, the temptation is to optimize a cheaper stand-in, but the stand-in quietly changes the objective.** FIDDLE's contribution is showing that you don't have to settle. With the right surrogate, you can make the honest metric cheap enough to optimize directly, and doing so produces measurably more reliable circuits. As quantum hardware scales and circuits get deeper, fidelity-aware compilation stops being a nicety and starts being the difference between a computation that works and one that drowns in noise.
